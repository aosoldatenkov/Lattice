# Vinberg's algorithm: the current implementation

This note describes how `lattice.vinberg` and the C++ module `vsearch_cpp` implement Vinberg's
algorithm: the coordinates adapted to the base point, the enumeration of roots by Fincke–Pohst,
the selection of walls, and the termination test. It also contains a proof that the rejection
step is valid regardless of the order in which roots are found (§5). The theoretical background
is in Vinberg's papers listed in [bibliography.md](bibliography.md) (in particular the 1972 paper
and the 1985 survey "Hyperbolic reflection groups").

| Experimental extra walls from cusps and 2-dimensional faces | `src/lattice/vinberg/walls.py` (`cusp_walls`, `geodesic_walls`, `RunTest`) |

## 1. Conventions

- $L$ is an integral lattice of signature $(1, n)$ with Gram matrix $A$, and $(u, v) = u A v^T$
  (vectors are row vectors of coordinates). Hyperbolic space $\mathbb{H}^n$ is the projectivization
  of the cone $\{x : (x, x) > 0\}$.
- A **root** is a primitive vector $r \in L$ with $(r, r) \neq 0$ such that the reflection
  $s_r(x) = x - \frac{2 (x, r)}{(r, r)} r$ preserves $L$, i.e. $(r, r) \mid 2 (r, x)$ for all
  $x \in L$ (`Lattice.is_root`, `is_root_core` in C++). Vinberg's algorithm uses the roots with
  $(r, r) < 0$; their mirrors $H_r = r^\perp$ are hyperplanes of $\mathbb{H}^n$.
- $W$ is the group generated by the reflections in all the roots with $(r, r) < 0$. The base point
  is a vector $p \in L$ with $(p, p) > 0$, and $C$ is the chamber of $W$ containing $p$ (when $p$
  lies on some mirrors, the chamber is fixed by an additional choice, see §3.3).
- In this convention (negative norms of roots), two distinct walls $H_r, H_{r'}$ of $C$, with
  $r, r'$ both pointing to the side of $C$, satisfy $(r, r') \geq 0$. This is the usual condition
  $(e, e') \leq 0$ for the outward normals in signature $(n, 1)$, since both the form and the
  orientation of the normals change sign.

**Norms of roots.** If $r$ is a root, then $|(r, r)|$ divides $2 \exp(L)$, where $\exp(L)$ is the
exponent of the discriminant group $L^\vee / L$; in particular $|(r, r)| \leq 2 \exp(L)$.
Indeed, let $d = \operatorname{div}(r)$ be the positive generator of $(r, L) \subset \mathbb{Z}$.
Then $(r, r)$ divides $2 (r, x)$ for all $x$, hence divides $2d$. The vector $r / d$ lies in
$L^\vee$, and since $r$ is primitive, its class in $L^\vee / L$ has order exactly $d$, so
$d \mid \exp(L)$.

**Distance to a wall.** For $(p, p) > 0$ and $(r, r) < 0$,
$$\sinh^2 d(p, H_r) = \frac{(r, p)^2}{|(r, r)| \, (p, p)}.$$
Both the Python and the C++ code order roots by a quantity proportional to this, see §3.1.

## 2. Coordinates adapted to the base point (`Vinberg.__init__`, `Vinberg._init_basis`)

Given a base vector $p$ with $(p, p) > 0$ (made primitive), the driver builds the basis
$e_0, e_1, \ldots, e_{n-1}$ of $L$ with

- $e_1, \ldots, e_{n-1}$ an LLL-reduced basis of the orthogonal complement $p^\perp \cap L$, which is
  negative definite, so that the reduction is exact (`Lattice.complement`, `Lattice.lll`);
- $e_0$ = `axis`, a vector with $(e_0, p) = \operatorname{div}(p)$ (`Lattice.dual_vec`), shifted by the
  vector of $p^\perp \cap L$ nearest to its projection to $p^\perp$, so that it is short.

This is `Vinberg.reduced_basis`. It is a basis of $L$: for $x \in L$, $(x, p) = k \operatorname{div}(p)$ for
some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$, and $x - k e_0 \in p^\perp$. The reduction does not change the heights of the
roots, but it keeps the coordinates (and the bounds of §3.5) small. In all cases $(e_0, p) > 0$ is
required (and checked, also by `VSearchCpp`): the roots of positive height must have positive
products with $p$, see §5.

**Choice of the base point** (`Vinberg.list_bases`). Without a given base vector, up to
`n_candidates` (default 50) candidates are compared: the base point of the given basis, if
$e_1, \ldots, e_{n-1}$ span the complement of a positive vector (its sign is arbitrary, and $p$ is
replaced by $-p$ if needed; then the given basis is kept), and the primitive vectors with
$(p, p) > 0$ and small coordinates in an LLL-reduced basis of $L$. The candidate with the smallest
$$\frac{(p, p)}{\operatorname{div}(p)^2} = (p^*, p^*), \qquad p^* = p / \operatorname{div}(p) \in L^\vee \text{ primitive},$$
is chosen (then the smallest $(p, p)$). The reason: a root $r$ at height $h$ has
$(r, p) = h \operatorname{div}(p)$, and $\sinh d(p, r^\perp) = |(r, p)| / \sqrt{|(r, r)| (p, p)}$, so the walls
at distance at most $D$ from $p$ have heights
$$h \leq \sqrt{2 \exp(L)} \, \sinh(D) \, \sqrt{(p^*, p^*)},$$
and the number of heights to search grows with $(p^*, p^*)$. The distance $D$ to the farthest wall
is not known in advance. On Allcock's list (rank 3), running the algorithm from each of 10
candidates on a sample of 522 lattices (in two bases each) showed that this key chooses a base
point within 6% of the best candidate in total time, while preferring the base points on the most
mirrors (a vertex of the chamber) and the smallest $(p, p)$ were 1.55 and 1.9 times slower.
On the whole list with 50 candidates, the total time in the LLL-reduced bases went down from
1401 s (with 6 overflows) to 302 s, and in the Lorentzian bases built by `experiments/allcock.py`
it stayed at 384 s (375 s before): the running time no longer depends much on the basis in which
the lattice is given.

All the search runs in these coordinates. Let $M$ be the Gram matrix in this basis (`self.M`),
$C = (e_i, e_j)_{i, j \geq 1}$ its lower right block, which is negative definite, and
$\beta = (\beta_0, \ldots, \beta_{n-1})$ the coordinates of $p$. Since $e_i \perp p$ for $i \geq 1$,
$$(e_0, p) = \sum_j M_{0j} \beta_j, \qquad (p, p) = \beta_0 (e_0, p), \qquad
s := \frac{(e_0, p)}{\beta_0} = \frac{(p, p)}{\beta_0^2} > 0.$$

## 3. Enumeration of the negative roots

### 3.1 Height and the quadratic form on a slice

Write $v = h e_0 + \sum_{i \geq 1} x_i e_i$. The **height** of $v$ is $h$; it measures the product
with the base point: $(v, p) = h \, (e_0, p)$. Subtracting the component along $p$,
$v = \frac{h}{\beta_0} p + w$ with $w = \sum_{i \geq 1} \left(x_i - \frac{h \beta_i}{\beta_0}\right) e_i \in p^\perp$,
so
$$(v, v) = s h^2 + (x + b)^T C (x + b), \qquad b_i = -\frac{h \beta_i}{\beta_0} \quad (i \geq 1).$$
With $Q = -C$ positive definite,
$$(x + b)^T Q (x + b) = s h^2 - (v, v).$$
For a root of height $h$, $-2 \exp(L) \leq (v, v) \leq -1$, so the vectors to enumerate are the
integer points $x$ of the ellipsoidal shell
$$s h^2 + 1 \leq (x + b)^T Q (x + b) \leq s h^2 + 2 \exp(L).$$
The distance to the wall is $\sinh^2 d(p, H_v) = s h^2 / |(v, v)|$, so roots are ordered by the
key $h^2 / |(v, v)|$ (class `Rational` in C++, compared exactly by cross-multiplication). The
Python side uses the equivalent key $(v, p)^2 / |(v, v)|$ in the coordinates of $L$.

### 3.2 The Fincke–Pohst search (`FPSearch`)

`FPSearch(Q, b, lbound, ubound)` lists all integer vectors $x$ with
$\text{lbound} < (x + b)^T Q (x + b) \leq \text{ubound}$:

- $Q = R^T R$ (Cholesky, $R$ upper triangular). The coordinates are fixed from the last one
  down. At level $k$, with $x_{k+1}, \ldots, x_{n-1}$ fixed, the admissible $x_k$ form an interval
  around the center $z_k - b_k$; the interval is scanned from its top downwards.
- At the last level ($k = 0$), points inside the inner ellipsoid ($\leq$ lbound) are skipped by
  jumping to the other side of the center.
- A point found outside the ellipsoid only ends the scan of a level once it is below the center.
  Above the center, it can only be outside because the top of the interval was computed with a
  rounding error, and the scan continues. (Pruning there would skip the whole interval; with exact
  rational data such ties are frequent. This was a real source of missed roots.)
- No regularization is added to $Q$: replacing $Q$ by $Q + \varepsilon I$ biases all computed
  values upwards by $\varepsilon |x + b|^2$, and loses the vectors close to the upper bound.
- The coordinates are 32-bit ints; the constructor checks that
  $|x_i + b_i| \leq \sqrt{\text{ubound} \cdot (Q^{-1})_{ii}}$ fits, and raises `OverflowError`
  otherwise. A matrix that is not positive definite raises `ValueError`.

### 3.3 The search window and exactness

`VSearchCpp::init_fps` searches height $h$ with
$$\text{lbound} = s h^2 + 0.5, \qquad \text{ubound} = s h^2 + \text{bound}, \qquad
\text{bound} = \lfloor c \exp(L) \rfloor + 0.5$$
(`bound` is passed by `Vinberg._init_basis`). Here $c$ is the internal attribute
`Vinberg.bound_factor`, set to $c = 2$ in the constructor. It is meant for testing only: to search a
different window, set `V.bound_factor` and call `V._init_basis()` again, which restarts the search.
A larger $c$ only adds non-roots, which are rejected; for $c < 2$ some roots may be missed, so
`run(certify=True)` raises `ValueError`. The attainable values $s h^2 - (v, v)$ lie in
$s h^2 + \mathbb{Z}$, and the roots have values in $[s h^2 + 1, s h^2 + 2 \exp(L)]$, so both bounds
keep a margin of $0.5$ from every attainable value: the result does not depend on rounding as
long as the floating-point errors stay below $0.5$. The center $b$ and $s h^2$ are computed
exactly (GMP) and rounded once. Every candidate is then checked exactly in integer arithmetic
(`is_root_core`), so floating point can only affect completeness, never correctness. (With
`only_roots = false`, the lower bound is $s h^2 - 0.5$, which admits all $(v, v) \leq 0$.)

**Height 0 and the chamber choice.** The roots of height 0 are the roots in $p^\perp$; they form a
finite root system $R_p$, searched once with bounds $(0.5, \text{bound}]$. When $R_p \neq \emptyset$,
$p$ lies on the mirrors of $R_p$, and the chamber $C$ is fixed by a chamber of $R_p$: `RootSysCpp`
takes the positive roots with respect to the chamber vector (or a random vector, if the given one
lies on a mirror), sorts them by the value of that linear functional, and extracts the simple roots
`sroots` greedily: a positive root is simple unless it has a positive Euclidean product with an
already chosen simple root. (A non-simple positive root $r$ has a simple root $\alpha$ in its
support with positive Euclidean product $\langle r, \alpha \rangle$, and $\alpha$ comes earlier in
the order.) The simple roots are the walls of $C$ through $p$ (`base_walls` in Python); their key
is $0$. Equivalently, $C$ is the chamber containing $p + \varepsilon c$ for the chamber vector $c$
and small $\varepsilon > 0$.

### 3.4 Running the search (`VSearchCpp::run`)

Each of `num_threads` workers holds one height and its `FPSearch`; heights are assigned in
increasing order, and a worker takes the next height when its search is exhausted. In chamber
mode, a candidate $v$ is kept if it is a root and $(w, v) \geq 0$ for all simple roots $w$ at the
base point (the others are rejected, which is justified by §5). `run(output_size, batch_size)`
returns once `output_size` roots have been found or every worker exhausted its current height, so
a height can be left half-searched and resumed by the next call. `searched_height()` returns the
largest $H$ such that all the heights $1, \ldots, H$ have been searched completely.

### 3.5 Overflow safeguards

The C++ integers are 64-bit `long`. Instead of checking every operation, `update_coord_bound`
bounds, once per height and before its search, the coordinates of all the vectors found so far
(using the bound of §3.2 on the ellipsoid), and from these, in `long double`: all products
$u A v^T$ of found vectors (root test, chamber test, `update_walls`, reflections) and the
cross-multiplied keys $h^2 |(v, v)|$. The constructor also checks $(e_0, p)$, and the chamber
vector is checked against the roots of height 0. If a bound exceeds $2^{62}$, the search raises
`OverflowError`. Python integers beyond 64 bits are rejected by pybind11 (`TypeError`).

## 4. Selecting the walls

The selection is Vinberg's: going through the roots in increasing order of distance, a root is
**accepted** if it has non-negative product with all the previously accepted roots (including the
walls through $p$), and **rejected** otherwise.

- C++ (`VSearchCpp::update_walls`): goes through the found roots by increasing key (skipping key 0),
  accepts a root if $(w, r) \geq 0$ for all the previously accepted $w$, and then keeps only the
  accepted roots: the rejected ones are discarded for good.
- Python (`Vinberg.update_walls`): takes the walls returned by C++ (and possibly walls from
  `cusp_walls` / `geodesic_walls`), orients them so that $(w, p) > 0$, drops those with
  $(w, p) = 0$ or $(w, w) \geq 0$, merges them with the active walls, sorts by the key
  $(w, p)^2 / |(w, w)|$, and recomputes the acceptance starting from `base_walls`.

Roots are not found in the order of distance: the key is $h^2 / |(v, v)|$, so a root at a larger
height but with a larger $|(v, v)|$ can be closer, and heights are searched in batches, in
parallel, and possibly only partially. The next section shows that this does not affect rejection.

## 5. Rejection is independent of the order

**Proposition.** Let $r, r'$ be roots with $(r, r) < 0$, $(r', r') < 0$, $(r, p) > 0$, $(r', p) > 0$,
such that $d(p, H_{r'}) \leq d(p, H_r)$ and $(r, r') < 0$. Then $H_r$ is not a wall of $C$.

Note that $r'$ is only required to be a root, not a wall of $C$. Hence a root rejected because of
$r'$ can never be a wall of the chamber, even if $r'$ itself is rejected later because of a root
found afterwards. Consequently, **the roots may be listed in any order**, provided every root
eventually appears in the list: rejected roots can be discarded at once.

*Proof.* Put $\hat r = r / \sqrt{|(r, r)|}$ and similarly $\hat r'$. Recall that $C$ lies in the
half-spaces $\{(x, r) > 0\}$ and $\{(x, r') > 0\}$ of all the roots of positive product with $p$.

*Case 1: $|(\hat r, \hat r')| \geq 1$.* The mirrors $H_r$, $H_{r'}$ do not intersect in
$\mathbb{H}^n$ (they are ultraparallel, or parallel with a common point at infinity). With the sign
$(r, r') < 0$, the two half-spaces containing $p$ are nested: one contains the other, and the
boundary of the inner one lies in the interior of the outer one. If the half-space of $r$ were
the inner one, every geodesic from $p$ to $H_{r'}$ would cross $H_r$ first, so
$d(p, H_{r'}) > d(p, H_r)$, contrary to the assumption (this includes the case of equal
distances). Hence the half-space of $r'$ is the inner one: $H_{r'}$ separates $p$ from $H_r$,
so $H_r$ does not meet the chamber $C$, which lies on the side of $p$ of the mirror $H_{r'}$.

*Case 2: $|(\hat r, \hat r')| < 1$.* The mirrors intersect along $S = H_r \cap H_{r'}$, of
codimension 2, and $U = \langle r, r' \rangle$ is negative definite. The group
$\langle s_r, s_{r'} \rangle \subset W$ preserves the definite rank-2 lattice $L \cap U$, so it is a
finite dihedral group of order $2m$, $m \geq 2$; its mirrors (all mirrors of $W$) pass through $S$
and divide $U$ into $2m$ sectors of angle $\pi / m$. Use the Euclidean form $-(\cdot, \cdot)$ on $U$
and let $p_U$ be the orthogonal projection of $p$ to $U$; the products of $p$ with vectors of $U$,
hence the distances from $p$ to the mirrors through $S$, depend only on $p_U$:
$\sinh d(p, H_u) = |\langle p_U, \hat u \rangle| / \sqrt{(p, p)}$ for $u \in U$, i.e. proportional to the
Euclidean distance from $p_U$ to the line $u^\perp$ in $U$.

The angle $\varphi$ between $r$ and $r'$ is acute, since $\langle r, r' \rangle = -(r, r') > 0$, and
nonzero, since $r \neq r'$. The point $p_U$ lies in the cone
$K = \{x \in U : \langle x, r \rangle < 0, \langle x, r' \rangle < 0\}$, of opening $\pi - \varphi > \pi / 2$.
$K$ is bounded by two mirrors, so it is a union of $k$ sectors, $k \pi / m = \pi - \varphi$, and
$k \geq 2$. Suppose $p_U$ lies in the sector of $K$ adjacent to $r^\perp$, at angle $\alpha < \pi / m$
from it. Its angle from $r'^\perp$ is $\beta = (\pi - \varphi) - \alpha$, so $\beta > (k - 1) \pi / m \geq \alpha$,
and $\beta < \pi$. The distances to the two lines are $|p_U| \sin \alpha$ and $|p_U| \sin \beta$, so the
assumption $d(p, H_{r'}) \leq d(p, H_r)$ reads $\sin \beta \leq \sin \alpha$; with $\alpha < \pi / 2$ and
$\alpha < \beta < \pi$ this forces $\beta \geq \pi - \alpha$, i.e. $\pi - \varphi \geq \pi$, which is impossible.
So $p_U$ lies in a sector not bounded by $r^\perp$. The chamber $C$ lies in the corresponding
dihedral chamber $D = \{x : x_U \in \text{sector}\}$, and $H_r$ meets the closure of $D$ only in $S$
(a line through the origin meets a closed sector of angle $< \pi$ not bounded by it only at the
origin). Therefore $H_r \cap \overline{C} \subset S$ has codimension 2 in $\mathbb{H}^n$, and $H_r$ is not a
wall of $C$. $\square$

**The roots through $p$.** The rejection against the simple roots at the base point (key 0, in
`run_chamber_mode` and through `base_walls`) has $(w, p) = 0$. Replacing $p$ by
$p_\varepsilon = p + \varepsilon c$, which lies in the interior of $C$ for small $\varepsilon > 0$, gives
$(w, p_\varepsilon) > 0$ and $d(p_\varepsilon, H_w) \to 0$, while $(r, p_\varepsilon) > 0$ and $d(p_\varepsilon, H_r)$
stays bounded away from $0$ for a root $r$ of positive height; the proposition applies to
$p_\varepsilon$.

**What the code satisfies.** All the rejections have the form of the proposition: the inequality
is strict ($(w, r) < 0$); `update_walls` compares a root only with accepted roots of smaller or
equal key (they are processed in key order); and all the roots of positive height have positive
products with $p$, since $(v, p) = h (e_0, p)$ with $(e_0, p) > 0$. The products between two
roots of positive height do not change if both are negated, but the chamber test against the
simple roots at $p$ does: with $(e_0, p) < 0$ the roots $v$ found would have to be replaced by
$-v$. (Earlier, `list_bases` could produce $(e_0, p) < 0$; the search then rejected the right roots
in the chamber test and never terminated, e.g. for $\langle 1 \rangle \oplus \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 1 \\ 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$.)

We have not found this observation in the literature. It matters in practice: the roots can be
enumerated in any convenient order (by height, in parallel, in batches), as long as every root
is eventually enumerated.

## 6. Acceptance and termination

**Acceptance is provisional.** Unlike rejection, acceptance depends on the order: an accepted root
is guaranteed to be a wall of $C$ only once all the roots at a smaller distance are known. Until
then, a root can be accepted because the closer root that would reject it has not been found
yet. The roots that are walls of $C$ are never rejected (§5), so once all the roots up to a given
distance are known, the acceptance among them coincides with Vinberg's procedure in the order of
distance, and the accepted roots up to that distance are exactly the walls of $C$ up to that distance.

**The finite volume test** (`Vinberg.update_rays`). `get_extremal_rays` computes, exactly, the
extremal rays and the lineality space of the cone $\{x : (x, w) \geq 0 \text{ for all active walls } w\}$.
The polyhedron is reported to have finite volume when the cone contains no lines and every
extremal ray $v$ has $(v, v) \geq 0$ (all the vertices are in $\overline{\mathbb{H}^n}$). When all the
active walls are walls of $C$, finite volume means that the polyhedron is $C$ (Vinberg's
termination criterion).

**Certified runs.** Since a root $r$ at height $h$ has $|(r, r)| \leq 2 \exp(L)$, its Python key
satisfies $(r, p)^2 / |(r, r)| \geq h^2 (e_0, p)^2 / (2 \exp(L))$. So all the roots with key at most
$K$ have height at most $\sqrt{2 \exp(L) K} / |(e_0, p)|$. `Vinberg.required_height()` computes this
bound for the largest key $K$ among the active walls. With `Vinberg.run(..., certify=True)`, a
finite volume is only accepted once `VSearchCpp.searched_height()` reaches the required height;
then all the active walls are walls of $C$, the polyhedron is $C$, and `Vinberg.certified` is set.
Without `certify`, the run stops at the first finite volume test that succeeds. This is enough to
show that a lattice is reflective, but the walls found need not be exactly the walls of $C$. For
example, for $\langle 3 \rangle \oplus \langle -1 \rangle \oplus \langle -2 \rangle \oplus \langle -5 \rangle$
with $p = e_0$, a plain run stopped after searching the heights up to 21, while its walls required
the heights up to 69; the certified run confirmed the same 13 walls.

**Termination.** There is no guarantee that the algorithm terminates: for a lattice that is not
reflective, $C$ has infinitely many walls and the finite volume test never succeeds. The number of
iterations is bounded by the argument `iterations` of `Vinberg.run`.

## 7. Open points

- **Sign of the extremal rays.** The finite volume test checks $(v, v) \geq 0$ for the extremal
  rays, which describes both components of the light cone. A cone containing $p$ and a ray in
  the past component would contain spacelike vectors; checking also $(v, p) \geq 0$ would exclude
  this. It is not known whether this can happen for the polyhedra arising in the algorithm.
- `src/lattice/draw/render.py` and `experiments/render_examples.py` call `VSearchCpp` without the
  `chamber` argument (an older signature).
